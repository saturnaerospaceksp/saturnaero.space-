MNSWT

Martian Nuclear SaltWater Transporter

Mars Transport System

Built for sustained acceleration to Mars.

The Martian Nuclear Saltwater Transporter is Saturn Aerospace's primary interplanetary transport station for high-speed Mars operations. Built around continuously running nuclear saltwater engines, MNSWT is designed to keep accelerating through the transfer instead of relying on short chemical burns.

In its standard mission profile, the transporter can complete Earth-to-Mars transit in under four days, making it the backbone of our planned crew, cargo, and infrastructure movement between the inner planets.

MNSWT Full Stack

MNSWT is the transport station we use when speed matters, range matters, and the mission needs a propulsion profile that stays on the boil instead of stepping through short burns.

Transit

Under four days from Earth to Mars in the nominal transfer profile.

Propulsion

Constant-thrust nuclear saltwater engines for sustained interplanetary acceleration.

Role

Main Saturn vehicle for Mars crew rotation, cargo transfer, and station logistics.

Lunar Orbit / 2026

MNSWT To The Moon

We are working toward putting MNSWT in lunar orbit in 2026. That is the next step we want to make real, not just something we talk about. The idea is simple: build a station crews can actually use and trust, so the trip to the Moon starts to feel like a route we know how to make happen.

We want to send four Rhea vehicles to the station, then bring them down to the Moon and start the work that follows. That means power, cargo, habitat hardware, and all the small pieces that turn a plan into something people can actually live with.

It is a big target, and we are not pretending otherwise. But that is the point. MNSWT is there to make the distance shorter, the work steadier, and the next step feel less like a leap. One station, one transfer, one piece of the surface at a time, until the first city starts to feel less like a dream and more like something we are building for real.

Mission Focus

Keep the station moving, keep the crew supported, and make the path to the Moon practical enough to repeat.

What Comes Next

A working orbiting hub, four Rhea deliveries, and the first real construction steps on the lunar surface.
